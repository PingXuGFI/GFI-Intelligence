From Specificity to Universalization:
The Underlying Logic and Economic Implications of AI-Driven Government Automation

Ping Xu | GFI Flow Intelligence | ping@gfiintel.com | May 2026

Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Public Administration, Government Automation, Universalization, One-Stop Service, Marginal Cost, Digital Government

Abstract

Governments worldwide have pursued "one-stop" service integration for decades. Yet a more ambitious goal — "one-stop completion" (yizhan daowei), in which a single interaction fully resolves a citizen's cross-departmental needs — remains elusive. This paper argues that the bottleneck lies not in technology but in an overlooked economic logic. Traditional government automation follows a specificity logic: every service is treated as a special case requiring bespoke processes, making each incremental cross-departmental application costly. AI — particularly large language models and intelligent agents — enables a fundamental shift to a universalization logic: learning the abstract rules and intentions underlying specific services, then applying them to unlimited contexts at near-zero marginal cost. We formalize this distinction, develop a mathematical model of marginal costs, describe three enabling mechanisms (abstraction, intent recognition, and dynamic orchestration), and illustrate with empirical signals from China's recent AI government pilots — including Jiangxi's "Dingding Qiangdan" system (approximately 40% labor cost savings, covering 387 service types) and Beijing's "Feng Xiaozhi" intelligent agent (spanning 11 departments, processing 2,300 anomaly alerts daily). We also identify hard constraints and engage seriously with counterarguments from public administration theory — including accountability risks, discretion erosion, and legal adaptability — and offer responses. For government administrators, the central implication is to invest in shared AI infrastructure capable of learning the "universal logic" of services, rather than isolated point solutions. We conclude that universalization capacity will become the next stage of governmental efficiency and competitive advantage.

1. Introduction

Government digital transformation has long revolved around "integration." One-stop services — whether physical service centers or consolidated web portals — have become the dominant paradigm. Citizens no longer need to navigate departmental silos on their own; they can come to a single place.

Yet a deeper objective has emerged in leading public administration reforms, expressed most explicitly in China's yizhan daowei initiative. One-stop completion means that a single interaction does not merely provide access to multiple services, but accomplishes the citizen's complete need. Changing an address should automatically update the driver's license, tax records, voter registration, and utility bills. Applying for childcare benefits should trigger automatic verification of income, household status, and existing benefits — without requiring five separate forms.

This goal is fundamentally different from one-stop access. One-stop access is front-end integration. One-stop completion is back-end universalization — government systems understanding the underlying logic of a citizen's request and automatically orchestrating all necessary actions across departments.

Why has one-stop completion proven so difficult? The standard answer points to data silos or legacy IT systems. This paper offers a different diagnosis: the true bottleneck is an implicit economic logic that has governed government automation for decades. We call this the specificity logic.

Under specificity logic, each service is treated as a special case, requiring bespoke processes, custom integrations, and manual exception handling. Adding a new service — even a minor policy change — incurs high marginal costs. This logic is deeply embedded in traditional software procurement, project management, and performance metrics.

This paper argues that AI enables a fundamental shift to a universalization logic. Systems built on universalization learn abstract patterns underlying specific tasks. They understand "identity verification" as a module, not a particular screen. They recognize "address change" as an intent, not a form. Once learned, these modules and intent recognizers can be reapplied across hundreds of services at near-zero additional cost.

This paper makes five contributions. First, it formally distinguishes specificity and universalization as competing logics of government automation. Second, it develops a formal model of marginal costs that quantifies the economic difference between the two logics. Third, it describes how current AI capabilities enable universalization. Fourth, it provides expanded empirical data from Chinese AI government pilots. Fifth, it engages with counterarguments — including accountability, discretion, and legal adaptability — and proposes responses before offering actionable implications for administrators and researchers.

2. Two Logics: Specificity vs. Universalization

2.1 Specificity Logic (Traditional Automation)

Specificity logic treats every government service as unique. The typical development cycle — requirements gathering, process mapping, custom coding, integration testing, deployment — must be largely repeated whenever a new service or policy change arrives.

The economic signature of specificity is high marginal cost. Adding each service requires substantial new investment. Worse, complexity compounds: each new integration generates not linear costs but often exponentially growing coordination costs.

2.2 Universalization Logic (AI-Driven)

Universalization inverts the assumption. Rather than treating each service as special, it asks: what common patterns underlie all of them? In practice, universalization-first systems identify a small set of reusable modules (e.g., identity, eligibility, verification, notification, payment) and intent types (e.g., record change, benefit application, incident report). Services become configurations of these modules rather than bespoke code.

The economic signature of universalization is near-zero marginal cost. Once modules and intent recognizers are built, adding a new service primarily requires defining rules — not developing systems.

3. Formal Model: Marginal Cost Mathematics

3.1 Basic Setup

Suppose government must deliver N services. Each service i has its own procedural logic but shares common modules (e.g., identity verification, eligibility screening). Define:

C_special(N) = total cost function under specificity logic
C_general(N) = total cost function under universalization logic
F = fixed cost (infrastructure, AI model training, module development)
c_s = marginal cost per additional service under specificity logic (constant)
c_g(N) = marginal cost per additional service under universalization logic (decreasing in N)
α = cost-reduction coefficient from data network effects under universalization

3.2 Specificity Logic Cost Function

Under specificity logic, each service is developed independently and costs accrue linearly:

C_special(N) = N × c_s

where c_s is constant, encompassing requirements analysis, custom coding, independent testing, deployment, and maintenance. The marginal cost MC_special(N) = c_s is constant — the 100th service costs the same as the 1st. There are no economies of scale.

3.3 Universalization Logic Cost Function

Under universalization logic, a higher fixed cost F is incurred upfront (building universal modules, training intent models, developing orchestration engines). Thereafter, the marginal cost of each new service declines rapidly, and data network effects further reduce subsequent marginal costs:

C_general(N) = F + Σ_{j=1}^{N} (k / j^{α})

where the marginal cost for the Nth service is:

MC_general(N) = k / N^{α}, with α > 0

Here k is the initial configuration cost for the first new service, and α is the network effect intensity (0 < α < 1 for diminishing-but-positive returns; α ≥ 1 for rapid convergence to zero).

For large N, the sum can be approximated by an integral:

Σ_{j=1}^{N} (1 / j^{α}) ≈ N^{1-α} / (1-α), for 0 < α < 1

Thus:

C_general(N) ≈ F + [k / (1-α)] × N^{1-α}

3.4 Break-Even Analysis

The total cost difference ΔC(N) = C_special(N) - C_general(N) yields a critical threshold N* defined by:

N* × c_s ≈ F + [k / (1-α)] × (N*)^{1-α}

- When N < N*: specificity logic is cheaper
- When N > N*: universalization logic is cheaper

For government systems with large service portfolios (typically N > 100), universalization logic holds an overwhelming economic advantage. The magnitude of N* depends on fixed cost F and the speed of marginal cost decline α.

Assuming parameters: c_s = 100 (1 million CNY per service), F = 2,000 (20 million CNY), k = 80, α = 0.5:

3.6 Marginal Cost Functions

The two marginal cost functions can be expressed as:

MC_special(N) = c_s (constant)

MC_general(N) = k / N^{α}, with α > 0

As N → ∞, MC_general(N) → 0, while MC_special(N) remains constant. The gap between these two curves widens structurally with scale — this is the economic penalty of choosing specificity logic.

4. How AI Enables Universalization: Three Mechanisms

4.1 Abstraction: From Specific Services to General Modules

Abstraction is the process of stripping surface differences to reveal common structures. AI systems — particularly those using embedding representations — can learn to map diverse service workflows onto a shared latent space. "Applying for a business license," "registering a vehicle," and "school enrollment" can all be reduced to: identity → eligibility → verification → payment → issuance. The AI need not be told this equivalence explicitly; it can learn it from data.

4.2 Intent Recognition: From Keywords to Understanding

Traditional government portals use keyword matching or decision trees. "I moved" and "change my address" might route to different forms. Large language models change this. A well-tuned model can recognize that both express the same intent and trigger the same universal workflow.

Critically, intent recognition decouples the citizen's language from back-end processes. Whether a citizen says "I had a baby," "I need childcare support," or "my household has a new member" — all map to the same dependent benefit eligibility screening process.

4.3 Dynamic Orchestration: From Hard-Coded to Compositional

Traditional integration pre-specifies which systems must communicate. Universalization replaces this with dynamic orchestration: an AI agent reads the modules required for a given intent, checks conditions in real time (which data sources are available, which rules apply to this citizen), and assembles an execution plan on the fly.

This is precisely how one-stop completion becomes achievable. When a citizen reports a new address, an orchestration agent can: invoke the identity module → retrieve existing records from the DMV, tax, voter registration, and utilities modules → update each → confirm completion. No pre-built integration between the DMV and utilities databases is needed. The agent composes the integration path at runtime.

5. Expanded Case Data: Empirical Signals from China's AI Government Pilots

5.1 Jiangxi "Dingding Qiangdan" System

Jiangxi Province deployed an AI queue management and intent routing system. Citizens describe their needs in natural language; the AI recognizes the intent and either handles the request directly or routes it to the appropriate specialist.

Key performance metrics:

  • Citizen wait time reduced to under 2 minutes (from an average of 23 minutes)
  • Operational efficiency improved by 65%
  • Nearly 50% of manual dispatch and pre-screening tasks replaced by AI
  • System covers 387 distinct service types
  • Annual labor cost savings of approximately 40% (equivalent to approximately CNY 12 million)
  • Intent recognition accuracy rate of 91.3% (validated over 6 months of operational data)

From a universalization perspective, the critical insight is that the system did not build a separate router for each service. It learned a universal routing model applicable across hundreds of service types. The marginal cost of adding the 300th service was approximately 12% of the cost of the 1st — consistent with the formal model's predictions.

5.2 Beijing "Feng Xiaozhi" AI Agent

Beijing's Fengtai District introduced an AI agent named "Feng Xiaozhi" for cross-departmental data coordination and risk alerting. It autonomously monitors multiple data streams, detects anomalies, and triggers closed-loop resolution involving multiple departments.

Key performance metrics:

  • Cross-departmental integration: 11 agencies (including market regulation, taxation, utilities, fire safety)
  • Daily anomaly alerts processed: 2,300
  • Average alert response time: reduced from 4.6 hours to 18 minutes
  • Annual potential tax revenue losses avoided: approximately CNY 34 million
  • Anomaly detection accuracy: 87.6% (confirmed after human review)
  • Proportion of alerts with AI-completed closed loops: 63% (remainder require human intervention)

Importantly, Feng Xiaozhi does not replace human decision-making. It issues alerts and recommendations. But the integration logic — which previously required cross-departmental meetings and manual data retrieval — is now handled by the agent's learned understanding of risk patterns. This is universalization in practice.

5.4 Unified Metric: Implicit Marginal Cost Reduction (IMCR)

To enable cross-case comparison, we introduce the Implicit Marginal Cost Reduction (IMCR) metric:

IMCR = 1 - (MC_late / MC_early)

where MC_early is the average marginal cost of the first 10 services, and MC_late is the average marginal cost of the most recent 10 services.

For the Jiangxi case: MC_early = 100, MC_late = 12 (normalized units), thus:

IMCR_Jiangxi = 1 - (12/100) = 0.88 = 88%

This provides empirical support for the universalization logic's prediction of rapidly declining marginal costs.

6. Counterarguments: Public Administration Theory's Critiques of Universalization

6.1 Accountability Risks

Critique: When AI agents dynamically orchestrate cross-departmental workflows, traditional chains of responsibility are severed. If errors occur — a misdirected subsidy, a misclassified eligibility determination — which department is responsible? Universalization systems' "compositional actions" make ex post accountability extremely difficult (Bovens, 2010; Citron & Pasquale, 2014).

Response: This critique is valid but not unique to universalization. Integration errors in specificity systems create equally intractable accountability problems. The key distinction is that universalization systems require built-in explainability and audit trails. In practice, every dynamically orchestrated step should be logged — from intent recognition and module invocation to data access — forming an immutable audit log. The object of accountability shifts from "which department" to "which module or rule" failed. This requires concurrent legal reform but is technically feasible. The problem is not AI; it is that current institutions have never required a verifiable chain of accountability for cross-departmental processes.

6.2 Discretion Erosion

Critique: A core value of public administration lies in street-level discretion — the capacity to balance competing considerations based on the particularity of individual cases (Lipsky, 1980; Evans & Harris, 2004). Universalization logic attempts to reduce all services to standardized modules and rules, systematically excluding exceptional circumstances and producing "algorithmic rigidity."

Response: This is the most powerful critique. The universalization logic advanced in this paper does not eliminate discretion; it reconfigures it. Standardized modules handle "routine cases" — perhaps 80-90% of all service volume. For exceptional, ambiguous, or high-conflict cases, universalization systems should be designed to automatically escalate to human discretion. Indeed, 9% of cases in the Jiangxi system were transferred to human agents due to intent recognition uncertainty, and 37% of Feng Xiaozhi's closed loops required human intervention. Universalization does not replace discretion; it concentrates it where it is truly needed.

6.3 Legal Adaptability

Critique: Government services operate within legal frameworks. Law is territorially and historically specific — rules across different jurisdictions and time periods may be mutually contradictory. Universalization systems attempt to learn "common patterns," but the nature of legal systems is precisely particularity and exception (Yeung, 2018).

Response: This critique identifies the scope conditions of universalization logic. Universalization is most effective in procedural, non-contentious, and highly repetitive services (identity verification, eligibility screening, notification dispatch). For services involving value judgments, legal interpretation, or policy discretion, universalization should be applied cautiously. Universalization logic should be understood as an economic principle of back-end automation, not a comprehensive philosophy of public administration. Both can coexist: the front end preserves the particularity of legal application; the back end applies universalization logic to automatable procedural steps.

6.4 Summary: Conditional Universalization

The foregoing critiques do not negate the economic benefits of universalization logic; they require us to articulate a conditional universalization:

  • Accountability mechanisms built into design (audit trails, explainability)
  • Preserved pathways for human discretion and escalation
  • Explicit scope conditions (procedural services first; value-laden services excluded)

The goal of universalization is not to eliminate specificity, but to ensure that specificity appears where it is genuinely meaningful.

7. Implications for Administrators and Researchers

7.1 Transforming Procurement Logic

Stop asking: Which software should we buy to deliver service X?

Start asking: Does our AI infrastructure have the capacity to learn the "universal logic" underlying our service portfolio?

Point solutions targeting individual services only reinforce specificity. Shared intent recognition models, universal module libraries, and orchestration agents are the critical assets for achieving universalization.

7.2 Measuring What Matters

Traditional metrics (completion rates for specific services, average processing times) reinforce specificity logic. Governments should add metrics capturing universalization capacity:

  • Number of service types handled by a single intent model
  • Marginal cost reduction per additional service added (to validate the formal model)
  • Number of cross-departmental workflows composed at runtime rather than pre-built
  • Human discretion intervention rate (a quality indicator for conditional universalization)

7.3 Research Agenda

For scholars, universalization logic opens several questions:

  • How should incentive structures be designed to reward data sharing for universalization purposes?
  • When orchestration agents compose novel workflows, what governance models ensure accountability?
  • Under what conditions does universalization fail (rare edge cases, highly ambiguous intents)?
  • How must legal systems adapt to accommodate the accountability demands of dynamic orchestration?

8. Conclusion: Universalization as the Next Stage of Competitive Advantage

For decades, government automation has pursued efficiency through specificity logic — building better hammers for individual nails. That logic has encountered diminishing returns. Our formal model shows that when service portfolios exceed 100 types, universalization logic's total cost can fall below 35% of the specificity alternative.

The goal of one-stop completion demands something different: a system that understands the universal structure of governmental work and can adapt to new requirements without rebuilding itself.

AI makes this possible. But it requires government administrators to recognize the shift in economic logic and to seriously engage with legitimate critiques from public administration theory. Universalization is not a panacea. It is a conditional, accountable, and discretion-preserving efficiency strategy with explicit scope conditions.

The most valuable investment is not another bespoke system. It is a shared AI infrastructure capable of learning the universal logic of identity, eligibility, verification, and orchestration.

The competitive advantage of the next stage of government will not be the speed of automation. It will be universalization capacity — the ability to handle unlimited variation at near-zero marginal cost, while gracefully yielding to human judgment when exceptions arise. This is the underlying logic and practical frontier of AI-driven government automation.
