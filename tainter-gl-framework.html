38 Years Later, the Same Ruler

June 17, 2026 · GFI Flow Intelligence

In 1988, Joseph Tainter published The Collapse of Complex Societies. He asked a question that still haunts us: why do advanced civilizations fall apart?

His answer was simple and devastating. Complex societies don't collapse despite being complex. They collapse because they are complex.

Tainter observed that societies add layers of complexity to solve problems. Bureaucracy. Specialization. Infrastructure. Regulation. Each layer generates returns — at first. But the easiest problems are solved first. The remaining problems are harder. They require more complexity to solve. The returns diminish.

"In a situation of diminishing returns, collapse may be the most appropriate response."

At some point, the cost of maintaining complexity exceeds the benefit it produces. The system becomes too complex to simplify. And when an external shock arrives — drought, invasion, economic crisis — the system cannot adapt. Collapse is not a failure. Collapse is the last remaining form of simplification.

Tainter studied Rome. The Maya. The Chaco culture. He diagnosed ancient collapses. But he did not leave us a ruler.

The alignment is not accidental. Tainter was diagnosing the same thing — he just didn't have a ruler. He had to describe. We can measure.

When Tainter studied Rome, he found a society that had added layer after layer of bureaucracy, military expenditure, and administrative complexity. Each layer kept the empire running — but the cost of running it was consuming more and more of what it produced. By the time the external pressures arrived, Rome had no room to maneuver. It couldn't simplify because the complexity had locked itself into place. Collapse was the only way out.

Look at what we are seeing today.

  • UK ETA: GL 0.04–0.09. Five vendors. Each delivered their contract. No one verified whether citizens boarded their flights. The system was "complete" — and completely broken.
  • NHS NPfIT: GL 0.038. £12.7 billion spent. £10.1 billion net loss. The largest civilian IT failure in history — measured.
  • Birmingham Oracle ERP: GL 0.071. Section 114 bankruptcy. 187 processes at simultaneous go-live. The system was delivered. The outcome was not.

Tainter described the pattern. GL Framework measures it.

Rome could not simplify. The elites who benefited from the complexity resisted any change. The cost of maintaining the system grew faster than the system's ability to generate value. The same pattern appears in every one of these cases. The complexity exists because it serves someone. The someone resists removing it. The cost is transferred to someone else. The system degrades. GL moves toward zero.

"Collapse is not a failure. It is the last remaining form of simplification — when all other forms have been blocked by those who benefit from complexity."

What Tainter described as inevitable, GL Framework offers a way to measure and intervene.

  • You cannot simplify Rome. But you can ask: who verifies outcomes?
  • You cannot undo the complexity of Universal Credit. But you can ask: who is waiting, and for how long?
  • You cannot stop the OMB delay. But you can ask: who is accountable for verification?

This is what the GL Verifier does. It does not attempt to reform the whole system at once. It inserts a question that the system cannot answer — and makes that question visible. It measures the denominator that was never designed.

En 1988, Joseph Tainter publicó The Collapse of Complex Societies. Planteó una pregunta que aún nos persigue: ¿por qué se desmoronan las civilizaciones avanzadas?

Su respuesta fue simple y devastadora. Las sociedades complejas no colapsan a pesar de ser complejas. Colapsan porque son complejas.

Tainter observó que las sociedades añaden capas de complejidad para resolver problemas. Burocracia. Especialización. Infraestructura. Regulación. Cada capa genera retornos — al principio. Pero los problemas más fáciles se resuelven primero. Los problemas restantes son más difíciles. Requieren más complejidad para resolverse. Los retornos disminuyen.

"En una situación de rendimientos decrecientes, el colapso puede ser la respuesta más apropiada."

En algún punto, el costo de mantener la complejidad supera el beneficio que produce. El sistema se vuelve demasiado complejo para simplificarse. Y cuando llega un shock externo — sequía, invasión, crisis económica — el sistema no puede adaptarse. El colapso no es un fracaso. El colapso es la última forma de simplificación.

Tainter estudió Roma. Los mayas. La cultura Chaco. Diagnosticó colapsos antiguos. Pero no nos dejó una regla.
