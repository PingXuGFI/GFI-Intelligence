六十年眼镜产业的垄断围困与丹阳破局

——一个民生刚需市场的治理盲点与产业突围

视力矫正，是人类现代生活中最基础的医疗刚需之一。全球约40亿人需要视力矫正，其中25亿人尚未获得适当的视力保健。然而，这个关乎数十亿人“光明”的产业，却在过去六十年里，被一个隐形帝国从镜片、镜架、品牌到零售、保险全线垄断。

这个帝国的名字是依视路陆逊梯卡（EssilorLuxottica）——2018年法国依视路与意大利陆逊梯卡合并的产物。它用六十年时间，构筑了一个横跨全球的垂直整合生态系统，让消费者、竞争对手乃至各国政府都深陷其编织的“价格迷雾”之中。

一、垄断帝国：看不见的“光明税”

依视路陆逊梯卡的市场主导地位令人震惊：

  • 全球眼镜市场份额约30%–40%，某些细分市场甚至超过80%；
  • 年营收达285亿欧元（2025年），年产15亿片镜片；
  • 拥有雷朋（Ray‑Ban）、奥克利（Oakley）等自有品牌，为香奈儿、普拉达、阿玛尼等超过20个奢侈品牌生产镜架；
  • 运营近17,750家零售门店（Sunglass Hut、LensCrafters、Pearle Vision、GrandVision）；
  • 控制着美国第二大视力保险公司EyeMed。

这意味着：从验光、保险、镜片制造、镜架生产到最终销售，每一个环节的利润，都流向同一个口袋。哥伦比亚大学法学教授Tim Wu直言，其利润率“即使按奢侈品标准也堪称无耻”。

二、监管盲点：为何全球反垄断屡屡失效？

尽管垄断如此明显，全球主要反垄断机构却几乎无能为力：

国家/地区监管行动结果
欧盟2018年合并审查无条件批准，事后补救
美国消费者集体诉讼2025年驳回，法官称“大并不违法”
土耳其违反合并承诺调查罚款1700万欧元，结构未改
中国合并审查附加六项限制性条件

根本问题在于三个结构性盲点：

  • 定义困境：传统反垄断法依赖狭隘的“相关市场”定义，无法捕捉跨制造、品牌、零售和保险的全生态系统主导地位。
  • “大而不违法”谬误：将规模误读为成功，而非对竞争的潜在威胁。
  • 事后而非事前：监管永远落后于商业创新的步伐，罚款与补救无法改变结构。

三、中国对策：反垄断的“有条件批准”

中国国家市场监管总局是少数施加实质性限制的监管机构。调查显示，在中高端光学镜片市场，两者合计份额40%–45%；低端镜片批发市场，两者份额30%–35%，第二至第五名合计仅5%–10%。

中国最终批准合并，但附加了六项限制性条件，包括：不得强制搭售、不得限制经销商自主定价、不得拒绝向独立零售商供应等。这是全球少数具有前瞻性的监管干预，但其长期效果仍有待观察。

四、丹阳破局：产业集聚的“降维打击”

法律改革虽不可或缺，但终究缓慢且被动。对垄断更直接、更激进的挑战，正来自中国丹阳——这座人口不足百万的小城，年产树脂镜片超过4亿副，占中国产量的75%，全球产量的40%。拥有1600多家眼镜企业集聚一城，丹阳拥有无与伦比的成本与效率优势。

产业升维：从制造到智造

  • 技术突破：部分企业已开发出5度间隔的验光技术，打破了1843年以来通用的25度间隔体系，实现更精准的视力矫正。
  • 设备替代：攻克了高端自由曲面加工设备，成本仅为进口设备的一半。
  • 模式创新：与京东等平台合作，建立“产业带+电商直营”模式，绕过层层分销，直达消费者。
  • 透明化定价：通过线上“成本清单公示”，将出厂价、物流费、服务费逐一拆解，打破价格黑箱。

五、治理启示：从“管不住”到“管得了”

眼镜产业垄断与丹阳破局的案例，对全球治理至少有三点启示：

  • 反垄断不能替代产业活力：仅靠罚款和条件限制无法真正打破垄断，最有效的救济是让新的、高效的生产者能够直接触达消费者。
  • 信息对称是治理的前提：丹阳的“成本透明化”模式，从根本上瓦解了垄断者赖以生存的信息不对称。
  • 刚性需求的“公益属性”需要制度性保障：当基本民生需求被私人资本构筑垄断高墙时，政府必须有能力穿透“专业服务”的外衣，审视其背后的价格欺诈与结构不公。

结语：一副眼镜折射出的治理之镜

国家治理的成熟度，从不以GDP中垄断行业的贡献率来衡量，而以一个普通家庭是否能用合理价格配上一副清晰的眼镜来丈量。当垄断者比监管者更懂市场，当法律工具落后于商业创新，当“规模”被误读为“成功”——国家治理的第一性原理，便在这一刻失效了。

丹阳模式不是要替代全球分工，而是用极致的产业效率与透明的数字分销，向全世界证明：一副高品质眼镜的真正成本，远低于垄断者试图让你相信的数字。这副价值千亿美元的眼镜，折射出的不仅是光，更是全球治理体系的结构性盲点——以及中国产业升级所开辟的一条新路。

Sixty Years of Eyewear Monopoly and the Danyang Disruption

— A governance blind spot in an essential healthcare market and its industrial breakthrough

Vision correction is one of the most fundamental healthcare necessities in modern life. Globally, approximately 4 billion people require vision correction, of whom 2.5 billion lack access to adequate vision care. Yet for six decades, this industry—essential to billions of people's sight—has been monopolised from lenses, frames, brands, retail to insurance by an invisible empire.

That empire is EssilorLuxottica—the product of the 2018 merger between France's Essilor and Italy's Luxottica. Over sixty years, it has built a cross‑border vertically integrated ecosystem that has trapped consumers, competitors, and even governments in a dense fog of pricing opacity.

I. The Monopoly Empire: An Invisible "Vision Tax"

EssilorLuxottica's dominance is staggering:

  • Roughly 30%–40% of the global eyewear market, with some segments exceeding 80%;
  • Annual revenue of €28.5 billion (2025), producing 1.5 billion lenses per year;
  • Owns iconic brands like Ray‑Ban and Oakley, and manufactures frames for over 20 luxury houses (Chanel, Prada, Armani);
  • Operates nearly 17,750 retail stores worldwide (Sunglass Hut, LensCrafters, Pearle Vision, GrandVision);
  • Controls EyeMed, the second‑largest vision insurance provider in the United States.

This means every link in the chain—eye exam, insurance, lens manufacturing, frame production, and final sale—funnels profit into the same pocket. Professor Tim Wu of Columbia University has stated that its profit margins are "obscene even by luxury standards".

II. Regulatory Blindness: Why Global Antitrust Fails

Despite the obvious monopoly, antitrust authorities worldwide have proven largely impotent:

European Union2018 merger reviewUnconditional approval, ex‑post remedies

Three structural blind spots lie at the root:

  • Definitional paralysis: Traditional antitrust law relies on narrow "relevant market" definitions that cannot capture ecosystem‑wide dominance across manufacturing, branding, retail, and insurance.
  • "Big is not bad" fallacy: Scale is mistaken for success rather than a potential threat to competition.
  • Ex‑post, not ex‑ante: Regulation consistently lags behind commercial innovation; fines and remedies cannot change the structure.

III. China's Response: Conditional Approval with Teeth

China's State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR) is one of the few regulators that imposed substantive constraints. The investigation revealed that in the mid‑to‑high‑end optical lens market, the combined share was 40%–45%; in the low‑end wholesale lens market, the combined share was 30%–35%, while the second‑to‑fifth competitors combined held only 5%–10%.

China ultimately approved the merger but attached six restrictive conditions, including: no mandatory tie‑in sales, no restrictions on dealers' independent pricing, and no refusal to supply independent retailers. This is one of the few proactive regulatory interventions globally, though its long‑term effectiveness remains to be seen.

IV. The Danyang Disruption: An Industrial "Disruptive Strike"

While legal reforms are essential, they are slow and reactive. A more immediate and radical challenge to the monopoly is emerging from Danyang, China—a small city of less than a million people that produces over 400 million resin lenses annually, accounting for 75% of China's output and 40% of the global total. With over 1,600 eyewear enterprises clustered in one area, Danyang possesses unparalleled cost and efficiency advantages.

Moving Up the Value Chain

  • Technological breakthrough: Some firms have developed 5‑degree step refraction technology, breaking the 1843‑based 25‑degree system for more precise vision correction.
  • Equipment substitution: Mastered high‑end free‑form surfacing equipment at half the cost of imported machines.
  • Model innovation: Partnering with platforms like JD.com to establish a "industrial cluster + DTC e‑commerce" model, bypassing layers of distribution.
  • Pricing transparency: Online "cost breakdown disclosure" that lists factory price, logistics, and service fees, dismantling the price black box.

V. Governance Lessons: From "Can't Regulate" to "Can Regulate"

The eyewear monopoly and the Danyang disruption offer at least three insights for global governance:

  • Antitrust cannot substitute for industrial vitality: Fines and conditions alone cannot break monopolies; the most effective remedy is to enable new, efficient producers to reach consumers directly.
  • Information symmetry is a prerequisite for governance: Danyang's "cost transparency" model fundamentally undermines the information asymmetry on which monopolists thrive.
  • The "public interest" nature of essential needs requires institutional safeguards: When basic livelihood needs are walled off by private capital, the state must be able to penetrate the veneer of "professional services" and examine the underlying price manipulation and structural injustice.

Conclusion: A Pair of Glasses as a Mirror of Governance

The maturity of state governance is never measured by the contribution of monopoly industries to GDP, but by whether an ordinary family can afford a decent pair of glasses at a reasonable price. When monopolists understand the market better than regulators, when legal tools lag behind commercial innovation, and when "scale" is mistaken for "success"—the First Principles of state governance fail at that very moment.

The Danyang model does not seek to replace global supply chains; it demonstrates, through extreme industrial efficiency and transparent digital distribution, that the true cost of high‑quality eyewear is far lower than the monopolist wants you to believe. This billion‑dollar pair of glasses reflects not just light, but also the structural blind spots of global governance—and a new path opened by China's industrial upgrading.
